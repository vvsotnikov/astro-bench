\abstract{We present astro-bench, an open benchmark for evaluating AI agents as autonomous machine learning researchers on real astrophysics data. Using the KASCADE cosmic ray experiment's detector array measurements, we task AI agents with two classification problems: 5-class mass composition and binary gamma/hadron separation. Each agent receives the dataset, a published baseline, and 50 evaluation attempts. We compare agents from multiple providers --- Claude (Opus~4.6, Sonnet~4.6, Haiku~4.5), GPT Codex, Qwen, and Kimi --- measuring both final performance (Best@50) and convergence trajectories (Best@$N$ for $N = 1, \ldots, 50$). On composition, the best agent achieves a fraction error of [TBD], improving upon the published result of 0.107. On gamma/hadron separation, the best agent achieves a hadronic survival rate of [TBD] at 75\% gamma efficiency. Beyond final metrics, the controlled experimental design enables analysis of research strategies, convergence speed, architecture diversity, and failure modes across agents. All code, predictions, and evaluation logs are publicly available.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The classification of cosmic ray primaries by mass group is a fundamental challenge in astroparticle physics. When a high-energy cosmic ray strikes Earth's atmosphere, it produces an extensive air shower (EAS) --- a cascade of billions of secondary particles whose properties at ground level encode information about the primary particle's identity. The KASCADE experiment~\cite{kascade_exp} operated a 200$\times$200~m detector array in Karlsruhe, Germany, for approximately 25 years (1996--2013), measuring electron/photon densities, muon densities, and arrival times across 252 detector stations.

Recovering the mass composition of cosmic rays at PeV energies is one of the key open questions in the field. The ``knee'' in the cosmic ray energy spectrum at $\sim$3$\times$10$^{15}$~eV is believed to mark the maximum energy achievable by galactic cosmic ray sources, but the exact composition at and above the knee constrains whether this is due to rigidity-dependent acceleration limits or propagation effects~\cite{kascade_jcap}. Better classifiers enable better unfolding of the true mass composition from measured shower data.

Machine learning approaches to KASCADE classification have been developed over several years of expert iteration. Kostunin et al.~\cite{kascade_icrc} introduced Random Forest methods for both composition and gamma-ray separation. Kuznetsov et al.~\cite{kascade_jinst} systematically compared CNN, MLP, and RF architectures, achieving $\sim$51\% accuracy with a LeNet-style CNN (36.6K parameters) using four reconstructed shower features (electron number $N_e$, muon number $N_\mu$, shower age, and zenith angle) combined with the 16$\times$16$\times$2 detector grid images. These results were subsequently used to extract energy spectra of elemental groups~\cite{kascade_jcap}, yielding a proton knee measurement at 4.4~PeV with 5.2$\sigma$ significance.

A parallel development is the rapid emergence of AI agents as autonomous software engineers and researchers. In February--March 2026, several teams demonstrated that AI agents can write production-quality code when given appropriate evaluation frameworks: Cloudflare rebuilt Next.js on Vite~\cite{cloudflare_vinext}, Anthropic compiled a C compiler from scratch~\cite{anthropic_ccompiler}, and OpenAI introduced the concept of ``harness engineering''~\cite{openai_harness} --- designing evaluation environments rather than writing code manually. Karpathy's autoresearch~\cite{karpathy_autoresearch} demonstrated AI agents running ML experiments autonomously overnight. Papailiopoulos~\cite{papailiopoulos_adderboard} showed that different AI agents follow fundamentally different optimization paths when solving the same problem, arriving at structurally distinct solutions.

In previous work, we explored AI agents for gamma-ray astronomy~\cite{kostunin_agents2025} and agent-based code generation for the Gammapy framework~\cite{gammapy_agents}. The present paper extends this line of investigation by deploying AI agents on a well-defined classification task with a standardized evaluation methodology, enabling direct comparison with published human-designed solutions.

We ask three questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can AI agents beat human-designed classifiers on real astrophysics data?
    \item How fast do different agents converge to good solutions?
    \item How do agents' research strategies differ --- and what can we learn from their trajectories?
\end{enumerate}

Unlike prior work that deploys a single agent model on a single task, we conduct a controlled comparison: each agent receives an identical starting point (dataset, baseline, instructions) and a fixed budget of 50 evaluation attempts. This enables direct comparison of both outcomes and search strategies across agent models.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. \Cref{sec:data} describes the KASCADE dataset and evaluation methodology. \Cref{sec:method} presents the benchmark design and experimental protocol. \Cref{sec:results} reports results across all agents and tasks. \Cref{sec:trajectory} analyzes convergence trajectories. \Cref{sec:discussion} discusses agent behavioral patterns, implications, and limitations. \Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Data and Evaluation}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The KASCADE Dataset}

We use QGSJet-II.04 Monte Carlo simulations of cosmic ray air showers, following the methodology of Kuznetsov et al.~\cite{kascade_jinst}. The simulation produces events for five primary particle types: proton (H), helium (He), carbon (C), silicon (Si), and iron (Fe), with class labels 0--4 respectively.

\begin{figure}[H]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_detector.pdf}
\caption{KASCADE detector grid representation. (\textbf{a})~Schematic of the 16$\times$16 binned grid, with active stations shown in blue ($\sim$85\% of cells are zero). (\textbf{b})~Example electron/photon channel (log1p-transformed). (\textbf{c})~Example muon channel, which is sparser than the electron channel. Iron primaries produce relatively more muons; protons produce more electrons.\label{fig:detector}}
\end{figure}

Each event consists of two data modalities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detector grid images}: 16$\times$16$\times$2 arrays representing binned electron/photon and muon densities across the detector array ($\sim$85\% of cells are zero due to the sparse nature of air shower footprints).
    \item \textbf{Reconstructed scalar features}: energy $E$ (log$_{10}$(eV)), zenith angle $\theta_z$ (degrees), azimuth angle $\phi$ (degrees), electron number log$_{10}(N_e)$, muon number log$_{10}(N_\mu)$, and shower age $s$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{fig_separation.pdf}
\caption{Distribution of $\log_{10}(N_e/N_\mu)$ for the five mass groups in the test set. The $N_e/N_\mu$ ratio is the single strongest discriminant: heavier nuclei produce more muons relative to electrons. Adjacent mass groups overlap significantly, particularly H--He ($d \approx 0.54\sigma$) and Si--Fe ($d \approx 0.22\sigma$).\label{fig:separation}}
\end{figure}

Quality cuts are applied following~\cite{kascade_jinst}: $\theta_z < 30^{\circ}$, $\log_{10}(N_e) > 4.8$, $0.2 < s < 1.48$, and $\log_{10}(N_\mu) > 3.6$. After cuts, the dataset contains 383,547 events, split 70/21/9\% into training, validation, and test sets using \texttt{torch.utils.data.random\_split} with seed~42.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric: Mean Fraction Error}

The key metric is \textit{mean fraction error} --- a physically motivated measure of how well a classifier recovers true particle fractions across random mixture compositions, following the methodology of~\cite{kascade_jinst} (Section~4.3, Table~1).

All 5-component fraction combinations on a 0.1 step grid summing to 1.0 are enumerated (1001 grid points). For each grid point, an ensemble of 5000 events is sampled from the test set with the specified fractions, and the absolute error $|f_\mathrm{true} - f_\mathrm{predicted}|$ is computed per class. The metric is the mean across all classes and grid points (fixed seed~2026). A perfect classifier scores 0; lower is better.

This metric directly measures what matters for physics: the accuracy of unfolded mass composition spectra. It differs from per-event accuracy in that it weights systematic biases more heavily than random errors --- a classifier that consistently over-predicts protons will score poorly even if its per-event accuracy is high.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric: Hadronic Survival Rate}

For the gamma/hadron binary classification task (Task~2), the metric is the \textit{hadronic survival rate at 75\% gamma efficiency} --- the fraction of hadronic background events that survive a classification threshold set to retain 75\% of true gamma events. Lower is better.

This metric is standard in gamma-ray astronomy, where the goal is to identify rare gamma-ray showers (signal) against an overwhelming background of hadronic cosmic ray showers. The test set contains 1,514 gamma events and 34,237 hadron events; agents must produce a continuous ``gamma score'' (higher = more gamma-like) rather than binary labels. At the threshold where 75\% of gammas are retained, a perfect classifier would have zero hadronic survival.

The published baseline from Kostunin et al.~\cite{kascade_icrc} achieves hadronic suppression factors of $10^2$--$10^3$ (survival rates of $10^{-2}$--$10^{-3}$) at 30--70\% gamma efficiency using Random Forest regressors on reconstructed features.

\subsection{Baselines: Reproducing Published Results}

For composition, we reproduce the published LeNet-based CNN from~\cite{kascade_jinst}. Our reimplementation in PyTorch (36,810 parameters) achieves $\sim$50.4\% test accuracy and a fraction error of $\sim$0.107, consistent with the published result (individual runs range from 0.1069 to 0.1085 due to training stochasticity).

For gamma/hadron separation, the published baseline~\cite{kascade_icrc} uses a Random Forest regressor on reconstructed shower parameters, achieving suppression factors of $10^2$--$10^3$ at 30--70\% gamma efficiency.

\section{Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}

Each agent receives an isolated task directory containing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{download\_data.py} --- downloads and prepares the dataset from S3
    \item \texttt{verify.py} --- evaluates predictions, auto-logs results, enforces the attempt limit
    \item \texttt{baseline/} --- a reproduction of the published baseline model (attempt~0)
    \item \texttt{CLAUDE.md} / \texttt{AGENTS.md} --- task description, data format, physics background, and generic strategy hints
\end{itemize}

The agent is given no architecture-specific suggestions --- only physics background (what the features mean, why the task is hard) and generic advice (``feel free to try everything, from linear regression to diffusion models and transformers''). The agent must discover effective architectures, training strategies, and feature engineering on its own.

\textbf{Attempt budget}: Each call to \texttt{verify.py} counts as one attempt, with a maximum of 50. The agent must provide a text description of each attempt (logged automatically to \texttt{results.tsv}). The baseline is pre-loaded as attempt~0, so the agent's first evaluation is attempt~1.

\textbf{Isolation}: Each agent run operates in its own copy of the task directory. Agents cannot see other agents' work or the benchmark infrastructure. The instruction document is compatible with multiple agent frameworks (Claude Code via \texttt{CLAUDE.md}, Codex and others via \texttt{AGENTS.md}).

Each agent is run on both tasks (composition and gamma), producing up to $2 \times 6 = 12$ independent experiment runs.

\subsection{Metrics}

For each agent run, we report:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Best@$N$}: the best metric achieved after $N$ attempts ($N = 1, 5, 10, 20, 50$)
    \item \textbf{Convergence curve}: Best@$N$ as a function of $N$, revealing search efficiency
    \item \textbf{Failure rate}: fraction of attempts that crashed or produced invalid predictions
    \item \textbf{Architecture diversity}: number of distinct architecture families explored
    \item \textbf{Cost}: total tokens consumed and wall-clock time
\end{itemize}

The primary comparison metric is \textbf{Best@50} --- the best result achievable within the fixed budget.

\subsection{Benchmark Design Rationale}

The benchmark design was informed by extensive pilot experiments conducted during development. Initial pilot runs revealed several critical issues that shaped the final protocol:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data pipeline alignment}: Early experiments used a different data pipeline (combined QGSJet-II + EPOS-LHC, different feature set, different train/test split) than the published baseline. This made results incomparable. The final benchmark uses exactly the published pipeline: QGSJet-II.04 only, same quality cuts, same split seed.

    \item \textbf{Float precision}: Storing data as float16 (for space efficiency) degraded composition fraction error by $\sim$0.5\%. The benchmark uses float32.

    \item \textbf{Post-processing pitfalls}: Differential evolution bias optimization improved simulation metrics but distorted predictions when applied to real experimental data. The benchmark prohibits post-processing to ensure models are directly applicable.

    \item \textbf{Evaluation leakage}: Selecting model checkpoints based on test set performance inflated results by $\sim$1\%. The benchmark instructions require validation-based checkpoint selection.
\end{enumerate}

These lessons are documented in the agent instructions to prevent agents from repeating the same mistakes.

\subsection{The Confusion Matrix Ceiling}

Pilot experiments during benchmark development revealed that the composition classification problem has a fundamental performance ceiling. All training strategies applied to the same CNN+Attention architecture converge to a narrow band of fraction errors (0.1057--0.1068), with improvement saturating regardless of optimizer, loss function, or augmentation strategy. The dominant sources of error are proton--helium and silicon--iron confusion, driven by overlapping $N_e/N_\mu$ distributions. Breaking through this ceiling likely requires additional input features (e.g., timing information) or fundamentally different representations.

Whether different agents discover this ceiling independently --- and how they respond to it --- is one of the behavioral questions this benchmark can answer.

\subsection{Agent Research Strategies}

[Full cross-agent behavioral analysis to be completed when all experiments finish.]

An early observation from the gamma task is a striking difference in \textit{research stability} between model tiers. Haiku~4.5 can execute individual experiments competently --- writing training scripts, loading data, calling the evaluation API --- but struggles with sustained iterative research over long sessions. In our experiments, Haiku frequently lost focus: launching parallel GPU jobs despite instructions to run sequentially, killing training runs prematurely (after 37 minutes, assuming they were ``hung''), writing elaborate monitoring scripts instead of waiting for results, and running the same experiment twice (wasting 2 of its 50 attempts on duplicates). The agent required 2--3 prompt repetitions to stay on track.

This suggests that the limiting factor for smaller models is not individual task capability but \textit{context management} --- the ability to maintain a coherent research plan across dozens of experiments spanning hours. This has practical implications for benchmark design: a fixed attempt budget (Best@50) may favor larger models not because they find better architectures, but because they waste fewer attempts on operational errors.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Agents operate fully autonomously within each run (no human guidance during experiments). However, the instruction document itself was refined based on pilot experiments --- agents benefit from lessons learned during benchmark development.
    \item Results are on simulation data. Transfer to real KASCADE measurements requires validating that models generalize across simulation--data differences.
    \item The 50-attempt budget is somewhat arbitrary. Different budgets may favor different agent strategies (e.g., breadth-first may need more attempts to converge).
    \item Agent interfaces differ across providers (Claude Code, Codex CLI, etc.), which may introduce confounds beyond model capability.
    \item Stochastic training means the same agent on the same task may produce different results across runs. Ideally, each agent--task combination would be repeated multiple times.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We present astro-bench, a controlled benchmark for evaluating AI agents as autonomous ML researchers on real astrophysics data. By giving each agent the same dataset, baseline, and evaluation budget (50 attempts), we enable direct comparison of both outcomes and research strategies across agent models from multiple providers.

[Results summary to be completed when all experiments finish.]

The benchmark design itself required significant iteration --- pilot experiments revealed critical issues with data precision, evaluation leakage, and post-processing artifacts that would have invalidated comparisons. These lessons are documented in the agent instructions, ensuring that the controlled experiments measure ML research ability rather than pipeline debugging skill.

We make the benchmark, all agent predictions, and evaluation logs publicly available at \url{https://github.com/vvsotnikov/astro-bench}. As AI agents become more capable, benchmarks that capture not just \textit{what} was achieved but \textit{how} --- and that enable fair comparison across agent models --- will be essential for understanding the role of AI in scientific discovery.
