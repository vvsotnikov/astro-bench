\abstract{AI coding agents are increasingly capable of autonomous ML experimentation, but their ability to conduct open-ended scientific research --- where evaluation requires domain expertise and results must transfer from simulation to real measurements --- remains poorly mapped. We design a controlled environment around cosmic ray classification tasks from the KASCADE experiment, with domain-specific metrics, and run multiple proprietary and open-weight frontier agents under identical conditions. All agents surpass published baselines within their first few attempts and converge to the same performance ceiling, determined by the available simulation statistics and physical overlap between particle classes. We systematically analyze where AI agents succeed and where they encounter limitations --- in model capability, in harness reliability, and in the experimental infrastructure itself --- and derive practical lessons for designing scientific workflows around autonomous agents. All code, data, agent logs, and results are publicly available at \url{https://github.com/vvsotnikov/astro-bench}.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

AI coding agents are becoming increasingly autonomous as both the underlying models and the harnesses that orchestrate them improve. This capability is being applied to increasingly open-ended tasks: from software engineering with mechanical verification~\cite{cloudflare_vinext,anthropic_ccompiler,openai_harness} to autonomous ML experimentation on well-studied benchmarks~\cite{karpathy_autoresearch,papailiopoulos_adderboard}. Yet AI capabilities are unevenly distributed --- the frontier is jagged across tasks, domains, and tooling. Agents that excel at optimizing standard ML benchmarks may struggle with scientific domains where evaluation requires domain expertise, training data has known biases, and results must transfer from simulation to real measurements. The shape of this jagged frontier in scientific research is largely unexplored.

In this paper, we map a portion of this frontier by deploying multiple frontier AI agents on cosmic ray classification tasks from the KASCADE experiment~\cite{kascade_exp}. KASCADE operated a 200$\times$200~m detector array in Karlsruhe, Germany, for approximately 25 years (1996--2013), measuring electron/photon densities, muon densities, and arrival times from extensive air showers (EAS) produced by cosmic rays. Machine learning approaches to KASCADE classification have been developed over several years of expert iteration: Kostunin et al.~\cite{kascade_icrc} introduced Random Forest methods for composition and gamma-ray separation; Kuznetsov et al.~\cite{kascade_jinst} systematically compared CNN, MLP, and RF architectures, achieving $\sim$51\% accuracy with a LeNet-style CNN; these results were used to measure the proton knee at 4.4~PeV with 5.2$\sigma$ significance~\cite{kascade_jcap}. In previous work, we explored AI agents for gamma-ray astronomy~\cite{kostunin_agents2025} and agent-based code generation for the Gammapy framework~\cite{gammapy_agents}.

We design a controlled environment with two classification tasks --- 5-class mass composition and binary gamma/hadron separation --- each with a single domain-specific metric, a published human baseline, and a budget of 50 evaluation attempts. Multiple proprietary and open-weight frontier agents run under identical conditions. We systematically analyze the results to answer:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Where do AI agents succeed and where do they encounter limitations --- in model capability, in harness reliability, or in the experimental infrastructure itself?
    \item Do agents from different providers converge to the same solutions, and does cross-agent diversity produce better results than any individual agent?
    \item What practical lessons emerge for designing scientific experiments and evaluation environments that can leverage autonomous AI agents effectively?
\end{enumerate}

We report both final performance (Best@50) and attempt-by-attempt research trajectories across agents from Anthropic, OpenAI, Alibaba, and Moonshot. Beyond the classification results, we document the challenges encountered and derive practical conclusions for multiple audiences: astrophysicists evaluating AI-assisted workflows, harness engineers building tools for autonomous research, and researchers designing the next generation of AI-ready scientific experiments.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. \Cref{sec:related} surveys related work. \Cref{sec:data} describes the KASCADE dataset and evaluation methodology. \Cref{sec:method} presents the experimental protocol. \Cref{sec:results} reports results across all agents and tasks. \Cref{sec:trajectory} analyzes convergence trajectories. \Cref{sec:discussion} discusses findings, implications, and limitations. \Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

AI coding agents have become increasingly autonomous as both the underlying models and the agentic harnesses that orchestrate them improve. This capability has been explored across software engineering~\cite{cloudflare_vinext,anthropic_ccompiler,openai_harness}, ML experimentation~\cite{karpathy_autoresearch,papailiopoulos_adderboard}, and end-to-end automated research~\cite{aiscientist}. In software engineering, a recurring pattern has emerged: humans design verification environments (test suites, compilers, CI pipelines), and AI agents write all the code. OpenAI termed this ``harness engineering''~\cite{openai_harness}. In ML experimentation, Karpathy's autoresearch~\cite{karpathy_autoresearch} demonstrated an iterative keep/discard loop --- modify code, train, evaluate, repeat --- that agents can execute autonomously overnight. Papailiopoulos~\cite{papailiopoulos_adderboard} showed that different agents develop structurally distinct solutions to the same problem, suggesting that agent diversity is itself a resource.

These advances raise a natural question: can the same approach work for scientific research? And if so, what are the unique challenges? In software, verification is mechanical --- tests pass or fail. In science, evaluation requires domain expertise: the metric must faithfully reflect scientific utility, the data pipeline must match published methodology, and the results must transfer from simulation to real measurements. Several benchmarks have begun exploring this space. MLAgentBench~\cite{mlagentbench}, MLE-Bench~\cite{mlebench}, and MLGym~\cite{mlgym} evaluate agents on standard ML tasks; RE-Bench~\cite{rebench} compares agents against human experts across time horizons; ScienceAgentBench~\cite{scienceagentbench} tests one-shot scientific code generation across four disciplines; PaperBench~\cite{paperbench} tests replication of published ML research. However, these benchmarks operate primarily in ML engineering, not in scientific domains where the evaluation function itself encodes domain knowledge.

In this paper, we investigate whether autonomous AI agents can conduct ML research in astrophysics --- a domain where evaluation metrics (hadronic survival rate, fraction error) reflect downstream scientific utility and where classifiers trained on simulated air showers must generalize to real detector measurements. We deploy multiple agents from different providers on the same tasks under identical conditions, enabling direct comparison of both outcomes and research strategies.

\section{Data and Evaluation}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The KASCADE Dataset}

We use QGSJet-II.04 Monte Carlo simulations of cosmic ray air showers, following the methodology of Kuznetsov et al.~\cite{kascade_jinst}. The simulation produces events for five primary particle types: proton (H), helium (He), carbon (C), silicon (Si), and iron (Fe), with class labels 0--4 respectively.

\begin{figure}[H]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_detector.pdf}
\caption{KASCADE detector grid representation. (\textbf{a})~Schematic of the 16$\times$16 binned grid, with active stations shown in blue ($\sim$85\% of cells are zero). (\textbf{b})~Example electron/photon channel (log1p-transformed). (\textbf{c})~Example muon channel, which is sparser than the electron channel. Iron primaries produce relatively more muons; protons produce more electrons.\label{fig:detector}}
\end{figure}

Each event consists of two data modalities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detector grid images}: 16$\times$16$\times$2 arrays representing binned electron/photon and muon densities across the detector array ($\sim$85\% of cells are zero due to the sparse nature of air shower footprints).
    \item \textbf{Reconstructed scalar features}: energy $E$ (log$_{10}$(eV)), zenith angle $\theta_z$ (degrees), azimuth angle $\phi$ (degrees), electron number log$_{10}(N_e)$, muon number log$_{10}(N_\mu)$, and shower age $s$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{fig_separation.pdf}
\caption{Distribution of $\log_{10}(N_e/N_\mu)$ for the five mass groups in the test set. The $N_e/N_\mu$ ratio is the single strongest discriminant: heavier nuclei produce more muons relative to electrons. Adjacent mass groups overlap significantly, particularly H--He ($d \approx 0.54\sigma$) and Si--Fe ($d \approx 0.22\sigma$).\label{fig:separation}}
\end{figure}

Quality cuts are applied following~\cite{kascade_jinst}: $\theta_z < 30^{\circ}$, $\log_{10}(N_e) > 4.8$, $0.2 < s < 1.48$, and $\log_{10}(N_\mu) > 3.6$. After cuts, the dataset contains 383,547 events, split 70/21/9\% into training, validation, and test sets using \texttt{torch.utils.data.random\_split} with seed~42.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric: Mean Fraction Error}

The key metric is \textit{mean fraction error} --- a physically motivated measure of how well a classifier recovers true particle fractions across random mixture compositions, following the methodology of~\cite{kascade_jinst} (Section~4.3, Table~1).

All 5-component fraction combinations on a 0.1 step grid summing to 1.0 are enumerated (1001 grid points). For each grid point, an ensemble of 5000 events is sampled from the test set with the specified fractions, and the absolute error $|f_\mathrm{true} - f_\mathrm{predicted}|$ is computed per class. The metric is the mean across all classes and grid points (fixed seed~2026). A perfect classifier scores 0; lower is better.

This metric directly measures what matters for physics: the accuracy of unfolded mass composition spectra. It differs from per-event accuracy in that it weights systematic biases more heavily than random errors --- a classifier that consistently over-predicts protons will score poorly even if its per-event accuracy is high.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric: Hadronic Survival Rate}

For the gamma/hadron binary classification task (Task~2), the metric is the \textit{hadronic survival rate at 75\% gamma efficiency} --- the fraction of hadronic background events that survive a classification threshold set to retain 75\% of true gamma events. Lower is better.

This metric is standard in gamma-ray astronomy, where the goal is to identify rare gamma-ray showers (signal) against an overwhelming background of hadronic cosmic ray showers. The test set contains 1{,}579 gamma events and 34{,}267 hadron events; agents must produce a continuous ``gamma score'' (higher = more gamma-like) rather than binary labels. At the threshold where 75\% of gammas are retained, a perfect classifier would have zero hadronic survival.

The published baseline from Kostunin et al.~\cite{kascade_icrc} achieves hadronic suppression factors of $10^2$--$10^3$ (survival rates of $10^{-2}$--$10^{-3}$) at 30--70\% gamma efficiency using Random Forest regressors on reconstructed features.

\subsection{Baselines: Reproducing Published Results}

For composition, we reproduce the published LeNet-based CNN from~\cite{kascade_jinst}. Our reimplementation in PyTorch (36,810 parameters) achieves $\sim$50.4\% test accuracy and a fraction error of $\sim$0.107, consistent with the published result (individual runs range from 0.1069 to 0.1085 due to training stochasticity).

For gamma/hadron separation, the published baseline~\cite{kascade_icrc} uses a Random Forest regressor on reconstructed shower parameters, achieving suppression factors of $10^2$--$10^3$ at 30--70\% gamma efficiency.

\section{Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}

Each agent receives an isolated task directory containing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{download\_data.py} --- downloads and prepares the dataset from S3
    \item \texttt{verify.py} --- evaluates predictions, auto-logs results, enforces the attempt limit
    \item \texttt{baseline/} --- a reproduction of the published baseline model (attempt~0)
    \item \texttt{CLAUDE.md} / \texttt{AGENTS.md} --- task description, data format, physics background, and generic strategy hints
\end{itemize}

The agent is given no architecture-specific suggestions --- only physics background (what the features mean, why the task is hard) and generic advice (``feel free to try everything, from linear regression to diffusion models and transformers''). The agent must discover effective architectures, training strategies, and feature engineering on its own.

\textbf{Attempt budget}: Each call to \texttt{verify.py} counts as one attempt, with a maximum of 50. The agent must provide a text description of each attempt (logged automatically to \texttt{results.tsv}). The baseline is pre-loaded as attempt~0, so the agent's first evaluation is attempt~1.

\textbf{Compute}: Each agent had exclusive access to a single NVIDIA Quadro RTX~8000 GPU (48~GB VRAM). The benchmark is limited by the number of evaluation attempts (50), not by wall-clock time; agents were free to train for as long as needed per attempt.

\textbf{Isolation}: Each agent run operates in its own copy of the task directory. Agents cannot see other agents' work or the benchmark infrastructure. The instruction document is compatible with multiple agent frameworks (Claude Code via \texttt{CLAUDE.md}, Codex and others via \texttt{AGENTS.md}).

\subsection{Metrics}

For each agent run, we report:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Best@$N$}: the best metric achieved after $N$ attempts ($N = 1, 5, 10, 20, 50$)
    \item \textbf{Convergence curve}: Best@$N$ as a function of $N$, revealing search efficiency
    \item \textbf{Research trajectory}: attempt-by-attempt log of architectures tried, with improvements and failures
\end{itemize}

The primary comparison metric is \textbf{Best@50} --- the best result achievable within the fixed budget.

\subsection{Benchmark Design Rationale}

The benchmark design was informed by extensive pilot experiments conducted during development. Initial pilot runs revealed several critical issues that shaped the final protocol:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data pipeline alignment}: Early experiments used a different data pipeline (combined QGSJet-II + EPOS-LHC, different feature set, different train/test split) than the published baseline. This made results incomparable. The final benchmark uses exactly the published pipeline: QGSJet-II.04 only, same quality cuts, same split seed.

    \item \textbf{Float precision}: Storing data as float16 (for space efficiency) degraded composition fraction error by $\sim$0.5\%. The benchmark uses float32.

    \item \textbf{Post-processing pitfalls}: The benchmark prohibits post-processing (e.g., per-class bias optimization) to ensure models are directly applicable to real data (see \Cref{sec:discussion} for details).

    \item \textbf{Evaluation leakage}: Selecting model checkpoints based on test set performance inflated results by $\sim$1\%. The benchmark instructions require validation-based checkpoint selection.
\end{enumerate}

These lessons are documented in the agent instructions to prevent agents from repeating the same mistakes.

\subsection{Gamma/Hadron Separation}

\Cref{fig:convergence_gamma} shows the convergence curves for all agents on the gamma/hadron task. Each agent receives identical starting conditions: the same dataset, baseline model, instructions, and 50-attempt budget.

Sonnet~4.6 achieved $3.79 \times 10^{-4}$ hadronic survival at 75\% gamma efficiency after 50 attempts---a $26\times$ improvement over the published random forest baseline ($\sim 10^{-2}$). The agent's trajectory shows three phases: rapid initial improvement (4 improvements in 5 attempts) reaching $4.38 \times 10^{-4}$ via ensemble of three CNN variants; a long plateau broken at attempt~25 through physics-motivated consistency features (muon/electron consistency, centroid distance); and a final improvement at attempt~47 using test-time augmentation (D4 symmetry group) on the best single model. Across 50 attempts, the agent explored CNNs, ResNets, transformers, gradient boosting, stacking meta-learners, and various ensembling strategies.

Opus~4.6 achieved $2.63 \times 10^{-4}$ (9~hadrons) after 50 attempts---a $38\times$ improvement over the baseline. Opus converged fastest, reaching its best result by attempt~10 with a 3-seed dual-branch CNN using squeeze-and-excitation attention, focal loss, and geometric mean ensembling. The remaining 40 attempts exhaustively explored alternative architectures (Vision Transformers, muon-focused CNNs, triple-branch networks), training strategies (MixUp, SWA, distribution-matched validation), and ensembling methods (arithmetic, harmonic, trimmed mean, logit averaging, stacking GBT on CNN embeddings)---none of which improved on the initial result.

GPT-5.4 achieved $2.92 \times 10^{-4}$ (10~hadrons) after 50 attempts---a $34\times$ improvement. GPT took a distinctly different path: it discovered pairwise ranking loss and asymmetric focal loss early (attempts 9 and 18), then spent the remaining budget on increasingly sophisticated gated ensembles combining full-pool and in-domain focal CNNs with feature-based routing. Its best result came at attempt~31 via a feature-gated ensemble. Notably, GPT was the only agent to explore domain-classifier-weighted ensembles and tail-specific loss functions.

Kimi~K2.5 achieved $4.09 \times 10^{-4}$ (14~hadrons) after 50 attempts---a $24\times$ improvement. Kimi employed a distinctive breadth-first strategy: rather than iterating on architecture or loss functions, it trained large pools of CNNs (up to 30 seeds of the same architecture) and submitted multiple top-$K$ selections per pool. This brute-force approach reached the Poisson noise band but converged more slowly than the other agents, with its best result arriving at attempt~40. In a separate run using the experimental KimiClaw harness (OpenClaw-based, browser-hosted), the same Kimi~K2.5 model achieved $3.21 \times 10^{-4}$ (11~hadrons) in only 7 attempts before the session crashed, reaching this level through test-time augmentation on a deeper CNN.

Qwen~3.6-Plus achieved $3.50 \times 10^{-4}$ (12~hadrons) after 23 attempts. Qwen built diverse ResNet ensembles with varied hyperparameters (weight decay, focal loss gamma), discovering that asymmetric loss functions and architectural diversity within the ensemble were more effective than increasing ensemble size.

\paragraph{Test set saturation.} The gamma test set contains 34{,}267 hadrons after quality cuts (zenith angle $\theta < 30^\circ$, $\log_{10} N_e > 4.8$, $0.2 < s < 1.48$, following~\cite{kascade_jinst}). At the performance level reached by the agents, the metric is determined by the count of individual surviving hadrons: Opus's best of $2.63 \times 10^{-4}$ corresponds to 9~hadrons, GPT-5.4's $2.92 \times 10^{-4}$ to 10, Sonnet's $3.79 \times 10^{-4}$ to 13, and Kimi's $4.09 \times 10^{-4}$ to 14~hadrons out of 34{,}267. The metric is quantized in steps of $1/34{,}267 \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-5}$, and the Poisson uncertainty on 9 events is $\sqrt{9} = 3$~hadrons (green band in \Cref{fig:convergence_gamma}). All four agents' results fall within the same $\pm 1\sigma$ band and are statistically indistinguishable. The gamma benchmark is effectively saturated at the resolution of the available Monte Carlo statistics.

\subsection{Composition (5-class)}

Unlike the gamma task, which saturated at the Poisson noise floor, the composition metric is continuous over 115{,}066 test events and improvements remain resolvable. All agents converged to fraction errors of 0.1051--0.1063, a narrow band that appears to reflect a confusion matrix ceiling driven by overlapping $N_e/N_\mu$ distributions between adjacent mass groups.

Opus~4.6 achieved the best composition result ($0.1051$) after 50 attempts. Its key innovation was a differentiable batch fraction matching loss that penalizes discrepancies between predicted and true class fractions within each training batch. Combined with a ResNet+MLP fusion architecture, 11 engineered features, and 12-way TTA, this recipe plateaued at $0.1053$ by attempt~8. Opus then explored broadly --- ViT, ConvNeXt, knowledge distillation, stacking with GBT, snapshot ensembles --- before breaking through at attempt~37 with a deep feature MLP using BatchNorm that matched the ensemble performance as a single model.

GPT-5.4 achieved $0.1052$ after 50 attempts using a single CNN+MLP fusion architecture for all 50 attempts, never changing the backbone. Its strategy was extreme depth-first optimization: an ordinal auxiliary head encoding the mass ordering (H$<$He$<$C$<$Si$<$Fe), a batch composition penalty, and full inverse-frequency class-balanced sampling (the single most impactful change, at attempt~12). The remaining 38 attempts were spent on fine-grained one-variable-at-a-time hyperparameter ablation. The final improvement at attempt~47 came from adding a composition regularizer specifically to the image auxiliary branch at weight 0.03.

Sonnet~4.6 achieved $0.1054$ after 50 attempts with three distinct phases: initial architecture search (attempts 1--4) establishing a LeNet+BN baseline at $0.1060$; a training strategy phase (attempts 10--27) where SWA and FiLM conditioning on energy pushed to $0.1056$; and a prolonged plateau broken only at attempts 49--50 through an Earth Mover's Distance ordinal loss that penalizes predictions far apart in the mass ordering.

\section{Convergence and Trajectory Analysis}
\label{sec:trajectory}

Beyond Best@50 scores, the \textit{path} each agent takes is itself data. The attempt-by-attempt logs in \texttt{results.tsv} enable fine-grained analysis of research strategies.

\subsection{Convergence Curves}

\Cref{fig:convergence_gamma,fig:convergence_comp} show the Best@$N$ convergence curves for all agents on both tasks. On gamma, all agents reach the test set noise floor (\Cref{sec:results}) within 10--30 attempts. Convergence speed varies: Opus reaches its best at attempt~10, GPT at~31, Sonnet at~47. On composition, agents converge to the 0.105--0.106 range within 10--20 attempts, with marginal gains thereafter.

\subsection{Architecture Diversity}

Despite arriving at similar final performance, agents explored markedly different architectures and training strategies. On the gamma task, Opus discovered dual-branch CNNs with squeeze-and-excitation attention and geometric mean ensembling; GPT developed pairwise ranking losses and feature-gated domain-expert ensembles; Sonnet introduced physics-motivated consistency features and test-time augmentation using the detector's D4 symmetry; Kimi trained large pools of identical CNNs and searched over top-$K$ selections; and Qwen built diverse ResNet ensembles with varied hyperparameters. On the composition task, Opus used knowledge distillation from multi-model teacher ensembles and a pure feature MLP; GPT explored ordinal auxiliary heads with batch composition losses; and Sonnet discovered FiLM conditioning on energy and Earth Mover's Distance ordinal loss. The convergence of diverse approaches to a narrow performance band --- despite fundamentally different architectures and loss functions --- provides evidence that the performance ceiling is determined by the data rather than by any particular modeling choice.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Comparison with Published Results}

On the composition task, all agents improved upon the published fraction error of 0.107~\cite{kascade_jinst}, with best results of 0.1051 (Opus), 0.1052 (GPT), and 0.1054 (Sonnet). On the gamma task, agents achieved hadronic survival rates of $2.6$--$4.1 \times 10^{-4}$ at 75\% gamma efficiency, representing a $24$--$38\times$ improvement over the published random forest baseline of $\sim 10^{-2}$~\cite{kascade_icrc}. These improvements come from standard deep learning techniques (CNNs, ensembling, augmentation, focal loss) that were not explored in the original publications, which used simpler architectures (LeNet, Random Forest) without modern training recipes.

To validate that agent-built classifiers produce physically meaningful results, we ran the best composition model on all 1308 runs of the KASCADE archive (1,572,349 events after quality cuts with $\theta_z < 18^{\circ}$) and applied confusion matrix unfolding (\Cref{fig:real_spectra}).

\subsection{The Confusion Matrix Ceiling}

Pilot experiments during benchmark development revealed that the composition classification problem has a fundamental performance ceiling. All training strategies applied to the same CNN+Attention architecture converge to a narrow band of fraction errors (0.1057--0.1068), with improvement saturating regardless of optimizer, loss function, or augmentation strategy. The dominant sources of error are proton--helium and silicon--iron confusion, driven by overlapping $N_e/N_\mu$ distributions. Breaking through this ceiling likely requires additional input features (e.g., timing information) or fundamentally different representations.

Whether different agents discover this ceiling independently --- and how they respond to it --- is one of the behavioral questions this benchmark can answer.

\subsection{Cross-Agent Prediction Diversity}

To assess whether running multiple agents adds value beyond selecting the best one, we analyzed the overlap of surviving hadrons (false positives) across the three agents with completed 50-attempt gamma runs (Opus, GPT, Sonnet) at 75\% gamma efficiency.

Of the 34{,}267 test hadrons, only 17 unique hadrons survive any of the three agents' classifiers. Of these, 4~hadrons survive all three agents' classifiers, 7~survive exactly two, and 6~survive only one (\Cref{tab:overlap}). The 4~irreducible events share a common physical signature: high $\log_{10}(N_e/N_\mu)$ ratios (1.9--2.6), consistent with proton showers where a leading $\pi^0$ produces an electromagnetic-dominated cascade that mimics a gamma-ray shower.

Despite this diversity, a na\"ive cross-agent ensemble (averaging scores from all three agents) yields 11~surviving hadrons---worse than Opus's 9 alone. Geometric mean ensembling gives 12. The agents are diverse in \textit{which} hadrons they misclassify, but combining their scores shifts the decision threshold unfavorably.

The composition task shows a similar pattern: agents agree on 89\% of predictions, and majority voting achieves a fraction error of 0.1051---matching the best single agent but not improving on it. When agents disagree (11\% of events), each is correct ${\sim}38\%$ of the time, and in 24\% of disagreements none of the three agents predicts correctly. Per-class analysis reveals complementary strengths (e.g., Sonnet is strongest on iron classification at 71.7\% vs.\ 68.7\% for Opus, while GPT leads on silicon at 42.8\%), but these do not translate to ensemble gains on the fraction error metric.

The conclusion is consistent across both tasks: the value of running multiple agents lies not in cross-agent ensembling but in maximizing the probability that at least one agent discovers the best architecture and training strategy.

\subsection{Lessons on Metric and Reward Design}

The benchmark's evaluation methodology evolved significantly during development. Early pilot experiments used classification accuracy as the primary metric, which proved poorly aligned with the downstream scientific goals: hadron filtering for gamma-ray astronomy and spectral unfolding for composition. We replaced accuracy with task-specific metrics --- hadronic survival rate at fixed gamma efficiency, and fraction error over random mixture compositions --- that directly reflect scientific utility.

Even with task-specific metrics, we encountered subtle reward design pitfalls. Differential evolution (DE) optimization of per-class logit biases improved the benchmark metric by adapting predictions to the training simulation's class distribution. However, this adaptation is a form of overfitting to the Monte Carlo data: the biases tuned on balanced simulation distorted predictions when applied to real experimental data, where the true composition differs. The benchmark now prohibits post-processing to ensure models are directly applicable to real data. This illustrates a broader risk: AI agents will exploit any gap between the evaluation metric and the true scientific objective, and metric design must anticipate this.

\subsection{The Simulation--Data Gap}

When applying agent-built classifiers to 73 million real KASCADE events, we observed that the classifiers reject real hadrons approximately $2\times$ more efficiently than predicted by Monte Carlo simulations (820 events passing the gamma threshold versus ${\sim}1{,}450$ expected from the simulation-calibrated survival rate). This suggests that Monte Carlo simulations overrepresent ambiguous, hard-to-classify events relative to real data --- or equivalently, that classifiers trained on simulation generalize conservatively when applied to real measurements. While a favorable bias for background rejection, this simulation--data gap means that benchmark performance on Monte Carlo does not directly predict real-data performance, and limits the confidence with which agent-built models can be deployed for gamma-ray searches.

\subsection{From Benchmarking to Scientific Exploration}

This benchmark was designed for controlled agent comparison: identical starting conditions, isolated runs, competitive evaluation against a fixed metric. While this protocol enables fair comparison, it is fundamentally different from the workflow needed for AI-assisted scientific discovery. In practice, one would want agents to be aware of each other's results, to cross-pollinate successful techniques, and to explore broadly before optimizing deeply. The 50-attempt budget, while sufficient for saturation in our setting, may be too short for tasks that require agents to formulate and test genuine scientific hypotheses rather than iterate on known deep learning recipes.

We also found that the benchmark infrastructure itself required substantial engineering. The evaluation metric, data pipeline, quality cuts, and instruction document all underwent multiple revisions as we discovered edge cases, data precision issues, and evaluation leakage. These ``harness engineering'' challenges --- designing robust evaluation environments for autonomous agents --- are not specific to astrophysics and are likely to recur in any scientific domain where AI agents are deployed.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Agents operate fully autonomously within each run (no human guidance during experiments). However, the instruction document itself was refined based on pilot experiments --- agents benefit from lessons learned during benchmark development.
    \item The benchmark environment reproduces the published experimental setup as closely as possible, but cannot guarantee an exact match due to the complexity of the original data pipeline. Our reproduced baselines closely match the published performance (e.g., fraction error 0.1073 vs.\ published 0.107). However, most agents outperform the human baseline from their first attempt, which may indicate subtle differences in our setup rather than a genuine improvement. Comparisons between agents remain valid regardless, since all agents start from identical conditions.
    \item Results are on simulation data. Transfer to real KASCADE measurements requires validating that models generalize across simulation--data differences.
    \item The 50-attempt budget is somewhat arbitrary. Different budgets may favor different agent strategies (e.g., breadth-first may need more attempts to converge).
    \item Agent interfaces differ across providers (Claude Code, Codex CLI, Qwen Code, Kimi Code), which may introduce confounds beyond model capability. Not all harnesses expose token usage or cost data, preventing a fair cost comparison across providers.
    \item We were unable to collect consistent cost and token usage data across all agents. A standardized harness (e.g., OpenClaw~\cite{openclaw}) could enable fair cost comparisons but would disadvantage agents optimized for their native interfaces.
    \item Stochastic training means the same agent on the same task may produce different results across runs. Ideally, each agent--task combination would be repeated multiple times. More broadly, it is unclear whether a single Best@50 run is the most informative protocol: multiple shorter runs (e.g., $3 \times$ Best@16) could provide variance estimates and might yield better aggregate results by diversifying starting points. We encourage future work to investigate the optimal allocation of a fixed compute budget between run length and number of independent runs.
    \item Claude Haiku~4.5, the smallest model tested, proved too unstable for the 50-attempt protocol: it launched parallel GPU jobs, killed long-running training, wrote monitoring scripts instead of waiting, and corrupted its results file. Whether this reflects insufficient model capability, harness limitations, or both remains unclear.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We present astro-bench, a controlled benchmark for evaluating AI agents as autonomous ML researchers on real astrophysics data. We summarize our findings along three axes.

\subsection{Findings for the KASCADE Experiment}

All agents improved upon the published baselines on both tasks: $24$--$38\times$ improvement on gamma/hadron separation, and fraction errors of 0.1051--0.1063 on composition (vs.\ published 0.107). However, the KASCADE Monte Carlo dataset is at least an order of magnitude smaller than what modern classifiers require for meaningful performance discrimination. All agents converge to the Poisson noise floor on gamma (9--14 surviving hadrons, \Cref{sec:results}) and a confusion matrix ceiling on composition, limiting both inter-agent comparison and downstream deployment confidence.

\subsection{Findings for Astrophysics}

This work demonstrates that existing AI agents can autonomously produce competitive classifiers for astrophysical data, obtaining results comparable to years of expert iteration. However, the benchmark reveals tensions between competitive evaluation and scientific exploration (\Cref{sec:discussion}). Our protocol --- isolated agents, fixed budget, single metric --- enables fair comparison, but real scientific discovery requires a different paradigm: agents aware of each other's results, able to perform wide and deep exploration, and optimizing for metrics that faithfully reflect downstream scientific goals rather than proxy objectives.

\subsection{Findings for AI-Automated Science}

All frontier models --- both closed-source and open-source --- quickly saturated the benchmark. This suggests that for tasks of this complexity, the choice of model matters less than the choice of task and evaluation design. Several open questions remain, which we identify as directions for future investigation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Variance}: How much does the random starting point define subsequent results? A single Best@50 run cannot distinguish genuine model differences from run-to-run noise. Multiple shorter runs (e.g., $3 \times$ Best@16) could provide variance estimates at similar total cost.
    \item \textit{Prior knowledge}: We provided agents with physics-informed instructions (e.g., ``$N_e/N_\mu$ ratio is the strongest discriminant''). How much of the performance comes from these priors versus genuine scientific reasoning from the data? The published KASCADE papers are likely in the models' pretraining data, further confounding this question.
    \item \textit{Cross-agent collaboration}: Our protocol isolates agents. Would cross-pollination --- letting agents see each other's results or code --- improve outcomes? This is a known unknown that requires careful experimental design.
    \item \textit{Harness quality}: Current agentic harnesses (Claude Code, Codex CLI, Qwen Code, Kimi Code) are suboptimal for autonomous research, particularly for long training loops. KimiClaw crashed mid-experiment; Haiku~4.5 proved too unstable for the protocol. Harness reliability is currently a practical bottleneck alongside model capability.
    \item \textit{Benchmark design for AI}: Our benchmark was originally designed for human researchers and adapted for agents. Future benchmarks should be designed with AI-in-the-loop from the start: larger datasets, fast iteration cycles, robust single-metric evaluation, and guardrails against reward hacking.
\end{itemize}

We make the benchmark, all agent predictions, and evaluation logs publicly available at \url{https://github.com/vvsotnikov/astro-bench}. We present these findings not as definitive recommendations but as systematic observations from an early investigation in a rapidly evolving field. We encourage the community to build on this work, particularly in domains with larger datasets where the gap between benchmark metrics and scientific utility can be more carefully controlled.
