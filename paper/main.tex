\Title{AI Agents as Autonomous ML Researchers: Benchmarking on KASCADE Cosmic Ray Classification}

\abstract{We present astro-bench, an open benchmark for evaluating AI agents as autonomous machine learning researchers on real astrophysics data. Using the KASCADE cosmic ray experiment's detector array measurements, we task AI agents with two classification problems: 5-class mass composition (proton, helium, carbon, silicon, iron) and binary gamma/hadron separation. We deploy two agents --- Claude Opus~4.6 and Claude Haiku~4.5 --- across 9 independent runs totaling over 150 experiments. On composition, Opus achieves a fraction error of 0.1045, improving upon the published result of 0.107 by 2.3\%. On gamma/hadron separation, Haiku achieves a hadronic survival rate of $3.21 \times 10^{-4}$ at 75\% gamma efficiency through a cross-architecture ensemble --- an order of magnitude better than published baselines. We analyze the agents' research trajectories, revealing distinct behavioral patterns: Opus focuses on systematic optimization within promising architectures, while Haiku explores a broader diversity of approaches including novel ensemble strategies. All code, model weights, training logs, and reasoning traces are publicly available.}

\keyword{cosmic rays; mass composition; KASCADE; AI agents; machine learning; deep learning; autonomous research; benchmark}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The classification of cosmic ray primaries by mass group is a fundamental challenge in astroparticle physics. When a high-energy cosmic ray strikes Earth's atmosphere, it produces an extensive air shower (EAS) --- a cascade of billions of secondary particles whose properties at ground level encode information about the primary particle's identity. The KASCADE experiment~\cite{kascade_exp} operated a 200$\times$200~m detector array in Karlsruhe, Germany, for approximately 25 years (1996--2013), measuring electron/photon densities, muon densities, and arrival times across 252 detector stations.

Recovering the mass composition of cosmic rays at PeV energies is one of the key open questions in the field. The ``knee'' in the cosmic ray energy spectrum at $\sim$3$\times$10$^{15}$~eV is believed to mark the maximum energy achievable by galactic cosmic ray sources, but the exact composition at and above the knee constrains whether this is due to rigidity-dependent acceleration limits or propagation effects~\cite{kascade_jcap}. Better classifiers enable better unfolding of the true mass composition from measured shower data.

Machine learning approaches to KASCADE classification have been developed over several years of expert iteration. Kostunin et al.~\cite{kascade_icrc} introduced Random Forest methods for both composition and gamma-ray separation. Kuznetsov et al.~\cite{kascade_jinst} systematically compared CNN, MLP, and RF architectures, achieving $\sim$51\% accuracy with a LeNet-style CNN (36.6K parameters) using four reconstructed shower features (electron number $N_e$, muon number $N_\mu$, shower age, and zenith angle) combined with the 16$\times$16$\times$2 detector grid images. These results were subsequently used to extract energy spectra of elemental groups~\cite{kascade_jcap}, yielding a proton knee measurement at 4.4~PeV with 5.2$\sigma$ significance.

A parallel development is the rapid emergence of AI agents as autonomous software engineers and researchers. In February--March 2026, several teams demonstrated that AI agents can write production-quality code when given appropriate evaluation frameworks: Cloudflare rebuilt Next.js on Vite~\cite{cloudflare_vinext}, Anthropic compiled a C compiler from scratch~\cite{anthropic_ccompiler}, and OpenAI introduced the concept of ``harness engineering''~\cite{openai_harness} --- designing evaluation environments rather than writing code manually. Karpathy's autoresearch~\cite{karpathy_autoresearch} demonstrated AI agents running ML experiments autonomously overnight. Papailiopoulos~\cite{papailiopoulos_adderboard} showed that different AI agents follow fundamentally different optimization paths when solving the same problem, arriving at structurally distinct solutions.

In previous work, we explored AI agents for gamma-ray astronomy~\cite{kostunin_agents2025} and agent-based code generation for the Gammapy framework~\cite{gammapy_agents}. The present paper extends this line of investigation by deploying AI agents on a well-defined classification task with a standardized evaluation methodology, enabling direct comparison with published human-designed solutions.

We ask three questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can AI agents beat human-designed classifiers on real astrophysics data?
    \item How do agents search for solutions --- what do their research trajectories look like?
    \item How do different agents (models) compare in their research strategies and outcomes?
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. \Cref{sec:data} describes the KASCADE dataset and evaluation methodology for both tasks. \Cref{sec:method} presents the agent framework and experimental setup. \Cref{sec:results_composition} reports composition classification results. \Cref{sec:results_gamma} reports gamma/hadron separation results. \Cref{sec:trajectory} analyzes agent research trajectories across all runs. \Cref{sec:discussion} discusses agent behavioral patterns, implications, and limitations. \Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Data and Evaluation}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The KASCADE Dataset}

We use QGSJet-II.04 Monte Carlo simulations of cosmic ray air showers, following the methodology of Kuznetsov et al.~\cite{kascade_jinst}. The simulation produces events for five primary particle types: proton (H), helium (He), carbon (C), silicon (Si), and iron (Fe), with class labels 0--4 respectively.

\begin{figure}[H]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_detector.pdf}
\caption{KASCADE detector grid representation. (\textbf{a})~Schematic of the 16$\times$16 binned grid, with active stations shown in blue ($\sim$85\% of cells are zero). (\textbf{b})~Example electron/photon channel (log1p-transformed). (\textbf{c})~Example muon channel, which is sparser than the electron channel. Iron primaries produce relatively more muons; protons produce more electrons.\label{fig:detector}}
\end{figure}

Each event consists of two data modalities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detector grid images}: 16$\times$16$\times$2 arrays representing binned electron/photon and muon densities across the detector array ($\sim$85\% of cells are zero due to the sparse nature of air shower footprints).
    \item \textbf{Reconstructed scalar features}: energy $E$ (log$_{10}$(eV)), zenith angle $\theta_z$ (degrees), azimuth angle $\phi$ (degrees), electron number log$_{10}(N_e)$, muon number log$_{10}(N_\mu)$, and shower age $s$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{fig_separation.pdf}
\caption{Distribution of $\log_{10}(N_e/N_\mu)$ for the five mass groups in the test set. The $N_e/N_\mu$ ratio is the single strongest discriminant: heavier nuclei produce more muons relative to electrons. Adjacent mass groups overlap significantly, particularly H--He ($d \approx 0.54\sigma$) and Si--Fe ($d \approx 0.22\sigma$).\label{fig:separation}}
\end{figure}

Quality cuts are applied following~\cite{kascade_jinst}: $\theta_z < 30^{\circ}$, $\log_{10}(N_e) > 4.8$, $0.2 < s < 1.48$, and $\log_{10}(N_\mu) > 3.6$. After cuts, the dataset contains 383,547 events, split 70/21/9\% into training, validation, and test sets using \texttt{torch.utils.data.random\_split} with seed~42.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric: Mean Fraction Error}

The key metric is \textit{mean fraction error} --- a physically motivated measure of how well a classifier recovers true particle fractions across random mixture compositions, following the methodology of~\cite{kascade_jinst} (Section~4.3, Table~1).

All 5-component fraction combinations on a 0.1 step grid summing to 1.0 are enumerated (1001 grid points). For each grid point, an ensemble of 5000 events is sampled from the test set with the specified fractions, and the absolute error $|f_\mathrm{true} - f_\mathrm{predicted}|$ is computed per class. The metric is the mean across all classes and grid points (fixed seed~2026). A perfect classifier scores 0; lower is better.

This metric directly measures what matters for physics: the accuracy of unfolded mass composition spectra. It differs from per-event accuracy in that it weights systematic biases more heavily than random errors --- a classifier that consistently over-predicts protons will score poorly even if its per-event accuracy is high.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric: Hadronic Survival Rate}

For the gamma/hadron binary classification task (Task~2), the metric is the \textit{hadronic survival rate at 75\% gamma efficiency} --- the fraction of hadronic background events that survive a classification threshold set to retain 75\% of true gamma events. Lower is better.

This metric is standard in gamma-ray astronomy, where the goal is to identify rare gamma-ray showers (signal) against an overwhelming background of hadronic cosmic ray showers. The test set contains 1,514 gamma events and 34,237 hadron events; agents must produce a continuous ``gamma score'' (higher = more gamma-like) rather than binary labels. At the threshold where 75\% of gammas are retained, a perfect classifier would have zero hadronic survival.

The published baseline from Kostunin et al.~\cite{kascade_icrc} achieves hadronic suppression factors of $10^2$--$10^3$ (survival rates of $10^{-2}$--$10^{-3}$) at 30--70\% gamma efficiency using Random Forest regressors on reconstructed features.

\subsection{Baselines: Reproducing Published Results}

For composition, we reproduce the published LeNet-based CNN from~\cite{kascade_jinst}. Our reimplementation in PyTorch (36,810 parameters) achieves 50.3\% test accuracy and a fraction error of 0.1079, consistent with the published result of $\sim$0.107.

For gamma/hadron separation, the published baseline~\cite{kascade_icrc} uses a Random Forest regressor on reconstructed shower parameters, achieving suppression factors of $10^2$--$10^3$ at 30--70\% gamma efficiency.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Agent Framework}

We deploy two AI agents as interactive autonomous ML researchers using Claude Code, Anthropic's official CLI tool:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Claude Opus~4.6}: 1M token context window, the most capable model in the Claude family. Used for composition (5-class) experiments.
    \item \textbf{Claude Haiku~4.5}: 200K token context window, a smaller and faster model optimized for speed and cost. Used for gamma/hadron (binary) experiments and additional composition runs.
\end{itemize}

Each agent operates in the repository containing the dataset, evaluation scripts, and a structured instruction document (\texttt{CLAUDE.md}) that describes the dataset format and physics background, the evaluation metrics, artifact preservation requirements, and the experiment loop (think $\rightarrow$ build $\rightarrow$ train $\rightarrow$ evaluate $\rightarrow$ log $\rightarrow$ iterate).

Agents have access to standard tools: Python with PyTorch, NumPy, and scikit-learn; a single GPU (NVIDIA Quadro RTX~8000 or A6000); and the \texttt{verify.py} evaluation script. Each agent writes training scripts, runs experiments, analyzes results, and decides next steps autonomously, with occasional human guidance on strategic direction.

A key design principle is \textbf{artifact preservation}: every experiment --- including failures --- must produce versioned training scripts, log files, model weights, softmax probability outputs, and predictions. This enables post-hoc analysis of each agent's research trajectory and ensures reproducibility. A standardized evaluation utility (\texttt{eval\_utils.py}) handles metric computation, differential evolution bias optimization (for composition), and artifact saving for every experiment.

\subsection{Post-Processing: Differential Evolution Bias Optimization}

After training, we apply per-class logit bias optimization using scipy's differential evolution (DE) optimizer. Given a model's softmax probabilities $p_{ic}$ for event $i$ and class $c$, the optimized prediction is:
\begin{linenomath}
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_i = \arg\max_c \left( \log p_{ic} + b_c \right)
\end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
where $b_c$ are 5 bias parameters optimized to minimize fraction error directly. This bridges the gap between per-event cross-entropy training and the aggregate fraction error metric. DE is used because the fraction error is non-differentiable (involving argmax and bincount operations).

\subsection{Experiment Progression}

We conducted 9 independent agent runs across the two tasks (\Cref{tab:all_runs}). Each run was a separate agent session, sometimes spanning multiple hours.

For \textbf{composition}, Claude Opus~4.6 conducted the primary investigation through four phases: (1)~SOTA reproduction of the published LeNet CNN, (2)~architecture exploration (CNN+Attention+MLP, ViT, GNN, enhanced LeNet, gradient boosting), (3)~training strategy optimization (SAM, focal loss, SWA, knowledge distillation, TTA, log1p preprocessing), and (4)~attempted pre-training on 73M real KASCADE events. Claude Haiku~4.5 independently ran two composition sessions, achieving comparable accuracy but with more crashes due to implementation errors.

For \textbf{gamma/hadron separation}, Claude Haiku~4.5 conducted four successive sessions totaling 77 experiments. The research progressed from simple MLPs and Random Forests through CNNs, autoencoders, contrastive learning, SVMs, isolation forests, Attention CNNs, ResNets, and Vision Transformers, culminating in a cross-architecture ensemble that achieved an order-of-magnitude improvement over published baselines.

The CNN+Attention+MLP architecture consistently outperforms all others. It uses 5 convolutional layers with squeeze-and-excitation channel attention, followed by adaptive average pooling, concatenated with a 2-layer MLP on 6 engineered scalar features. At 731K parameters --- 20$\times$ larger than the published LeNet --- it achieves 51.2\% accuracy and 0.1055 raw fraction error.

The Graph Neural Network, which treats active (non-zero) detector stations as nodes in a spatial graph with $k$-nearest-neighbor edges, achieves competitive results (0.1058 with DE) with only 77K parameters --- demonstrating that the sparse spatial structure can be exploited without dense convolutions.

This suggests a \textit{confusion matrix ceiling} for the CNN+Attn+MLP architecture on this dataset: DE extracts nearly all available information from the model's per-class error patterns, and the remaining errors are intrinsic to the classification problem (overlapping feature distributions between adjacent mass groups).

\section{Gamma/Hadron Separation Results (Task 2)}
\label{sec:results_gamma}

The gamma/hadron separation task was tackled exclusively by Claude Haiku~4.5 across four successive sessions totaling 77 experiments. The agent's progression from na\"ive baselines to a cross-architecture ensemble achieving $3.21 \times 10^{-4}$ hadronic survival rate represents an order-of-magnitude improvement over published baselines.

\subsection{Architecture Exploration}

The agent explored a remarkably diverse set of architectures over the four sessions (\Cref{tab:gamma_archs}). Early sessions focused on tabular methods (Random Forest, MLP, SVM, Isolation Forest) and simple CNNs. Later sessions introduced more sophisticated architectures including ResNets, Vision Transformers, autoencoders for anomaly detection, and contrastive learning approaches.

\subsection{Cross-Architecture Ensemble}

The agent's most significant discovery was that ensembling models from different architecture families provides meaningful gains --- in contrast to the composition task, where same-architecture ensembles showed no improvement. The final ensemble combines three models with learned weights:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attention CNN: weight 0.70 (best single model, survival $3.50 \times 10^{-4}$)
    \item ResNet: weight 0.10 (survival $4.1 \times 10^{-4}$)
    \item Vision Transformer: weight 0.20 (survival $5.2 \times 10^{-4}$)
\end{itemize}

The ensemble achieves $3.21 \times 10^{-4}$, an 8.3\% improvement over the best single model. This success --- compared to the failure of ensembles on composition --- suggests that different architectures capture complementary signal patterns in the gamma/hadron discrimination problem, where the physics is more distinct (gamma showers have essentially zero muon content) and the decision boundary is sharper.

\subsection{Comparison with Published Baselines}

The published gamma/hadron baseline~\cite{kascade_icrc} achieves suppression factors of $10^2$--$10^3$ (survival rates of $10^{-2}$--$10^{-3}$) at 30--70\% gamma efficiency using Random Forest regressors. The agent's result of $3.21 \times 10^{-4}$ at 75\% gamma efficiency represents approximately an order-of-magnitude improvement, and at a higher gamma efficiency operating point. This larger improvement relative to the composition task (2.3\%) suggests that the gamma/hadron problem was previously less optimized by human researchers, leaving more room for automated exploration to find gains.

\label{sec:trajectory}

\begin{figure}[H]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_trajectory_gamma.pdf}
\caption{Chronological research trajectory for gamma/hadron separation (Haiku~4.5, kept experiments only). Only runs using the correct metric (hadronic survival at 75\% gamma efficiency) are shown. Note the log scale.\label{fig:trajectory_gamma}}
\end{figure}

Beyond the final results, each agent's \textit{path} to those results is itself data. Across 9 runs, the agents conducted approximately 154 versioned experiments, with each producing training scripts, logs, model weights, and probability outputs.

\subsection{Opus on Composition: Depth-First Optimization}

Over approximately 36 hours, Opus conducted 12 experiments on the matched pipeline (plus 45 earlier experiments on a mismatched pipeline that were invalidated). Key decision points:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data pipeline correction} (critical): The agent initially spent several hours optimizing on a data pipeline that did not match the published methodology --- using 5.5M events without Age as a feature, different quality cuts, and a different train/test split. After reading the published code, the pipeline was corrected to match exactly. All prior results were invalidated.

    \item \textbf{Confusion matrix analysis} (key insight): The agent computed the theoretical minimum fraction error achievable given each model's confusion matrix, finding that the actual scores were within 0.001 of the theoretical floor. This correctly diagnosed that post-processing was saturated and the bottleneck was the confusion matrix itself.

    \item \textbf{Systematic strategy sweep}: After finding the best architecture, the agent tested 9 training modifications. The discovery that SAM provides the best DE-optimized result (despite not having the best raw score) was non-obvious and required the agent to separately evaluate raw and post-processed metrics.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Haiku on Gamma: Breadth-First Exploration}

Over four successive sessions spanning approximately 7 hours total, Haiku conducted 77 experiments with a notably different research style:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Session 1} (3 experiments, 20~min): Started with Random Forest and MLP on tabular features. Used an incorrect evaluation metric (survival at 99\% gamma efficiency instead of 75\%), so results are not directly comparable. Stopped prematurely due to session timeout.

    \item \textbf{Session 2} ($\sim$10 experiments, $\sim$1~h): Explored CNNs, SVMs, physics-based baselines, and isolation forests. Still using the incorrect 99\% metric. The metric was corrected mid-session, but most results are not comparable.

    \item \textbf{Session 3} (23 experiments, 90~min): First session with the correct 75\% metric throughout. Focused on MLP variants with multi-seed training, regression losses (BCE), contrastive learning, and autoencoders. Best result $6.43 \times 10^{-4}$ with multi-seed MLP ensemble. Several approaches failed: autoencoder crashed, contrastive learning did not converge, reweighting hurt performance ($3.15 \times 10^{-3}$).

    \item \textbf{Session 4} (41 experiments, $\sim$4~h): The most productive session. Introduced Attention CNN ($5.84 \times 10^{-4}$), then added engineered features for a breakthrough ($3.50 \times 10^{-4}$). Also tried deeper variants (worse), multi-seed ensembles (worse than single best), pure CNN without features (worse, confirming features matter), ViT ($6.72 \times 10^{-4}$, later tuned to $5.55 \times 10^{-4}$), autoencoder with features ($5.55 \times 10^{-4}$), and ResNet ($3.80 \times 10^{-4}$). Final cross-architecture ensemble (Attention CNN 0.70 + ResNet 0.10 + ViT 0.20) achieved $3.21 \times 10^{-4}$.
\end{enumerate}

A distinctive aspect of Haiku's approach was its willingness to try unconventional methods (isolation forests, contrastive learning, autoencoders) even when they were unlikely to succeed. While none of these novel approaches produced competitive results, the breadth of exploration eventually led to the key insight that architecturally diverse models produce complementary predictions.

\subsection{Failure Modes}

Agent failure modes differed by model:

\textbf{Common to both agents:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{File overwriting}: Both agents initially edited training scripts in place rather than creating versioned copies. This was corrected by adding artifact preservation rules to the instruction document.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Opus-specific:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Optimizer hanging}: The differential evolution post-processing step occasionally hung due to the \texttt{polish=True} option triggering Nelder-Mead on models with flat loss landscapes. Fixed by switching to \texttt{polish=False}.
    \item \textbf{Pre-training scale issues}: An attempt to pre-train on 73M real KASCADE events failed due to memory constraints.
    \item \textbf{Pipeline mismatch}: Opus spent $\sim$24 hours and 45 experiments on a data pipeline that did not match published methodology before the error was identified. All results were invalidated.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Haiku-specific:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Session instability}: Haiku went idle every 10--15 minutes, requiring human nudging to resume work. This necessitated splitting work across 4 sessions.
    \item \textbf{Crash-prone implementations}: Haiku's code had more runtime errors (shape mismatches, OOM from oversized batches), resulting in several crashed experiments per session.
    \item \textbf{Mega-script batching}: Haiku tended to batch many experiment variants into single scripts, causing all experiments to fail together when one had a bug.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Comparison with Published Results}

On \textbf{composition}, the best agent result (fraction error 0.1045, Opus~4.6) improves upon the published CNN baseline (0.107) by 2.3\% absolute (2.1\% relative). The improvement comes from three sources: (1)~a larger model (731K vs 36.6K parameters) with channel attention, (2)~stronger augmentation (cutout and Gaussian noise), and (3)~DE bias optimization that bridges the gap between cross-entropy training and fraction error evaluation. Neither agent discovered fundamentally novel architectures, but the specific combination of SAM, augmentation, and DE was arrived at through systematic experimentation.

To validate the composition classifier on real experimental data, we ran inference on all 1308 runs of the KASCADE archive (1,572,349 events after quality cuts with $\theta_z < 18^{\circ}$) and applied confusion matrix unfolding (\Cref{fig:real_spectra}). Importantly, the DE bias correction must \textit{not} be applied when classifying real data: the biases were optimized on balanced simulation (20\% per class) and distort predictions when the true composition differs. Using raw model predictions (without biases) produces folded spectra that already show the expected proton-dominated composition. The unfolded energy spectra show clear mass group separation consistent with published results~\cite{kascade_jcap}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{fig_real_spectra.pdf}
\caption{Folded mass composition spectra from real KASCADE data ($\theta_z < 18^{\circ}$, 1.57M events) classified by our best model (CNN+Attn+MLP with SAM, without DE bias correction --- see text). The expected mass group ordering is clearly visible: proton-dominated at lower energies with heavier elements contributing at higher energies, consistent with published results~\cite{kascade_jcap}.\label{fig:real_spectra}}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_unfolded_components.pdf}
\caption{Unfolded energy spectra per mass component from real KASCADE data, using energy-dependent confusion matrix inversion. The proton spectrum shows the knee feature at $\sim$3--5~PeV, consistent with the published measurement of $4.4 \pm 0.7$~PeV~\cite{kascade_jcap}. Error bars reflect statistical uncertainty propagated through the unfolding procedure.\label{fig:unfolded_components}}
\end{figure}

On \textbf{gamma/hadron separation}, the best agent result ($3.21 \times 10^{-4}$ survival at 75\% gamma efficiency, Haiku~4.5) represents approximately an order-of-magnitude improvement over published baselines ($10^{-2}$--$10^{-3}$ at 30--70\% efficiency~\cite{kascade_icrc}). The improvement is both in the metric value and in the operating point (75\% vs 30--70\% gamma efficiency). This much larger relative gain suggests the gamma/hadron problem was less thoroughly optimized in prior work.

\subsection{The Confusion Matrix Ceiling}

The convergence of all composition training strategies to 0.1045--0.1050 after DE optimization suggests a fundamental limitation of the current feature set and architecture family. The per-class confusion matrix analysis reveals that the dominant source of error is proton-helium confusion (overlapping $N_e/N_\mu$ distributions with separation $d \approx 0.26\sigma$) and silicon-iron confusion ($d \approx 0.16\sigma$). Breaking through this ceiling likely requires either additional input features (e.g., timing information, though this is problematic for real data), significantly more training data, or pre-training on unlabeled data to learn better representations.

Interestingly, no such ceiling was observed for the gamma/hadron task. The agent continued to improve results throughout all four sessions, and the cross-architecture ensemble provided a meaningful 8.3\% gain over the best single model. This asymmetry may reflect the fundamentally different nature of the two problems: composition classification requires distinguishing particles along a continuous spectrum of $N_e/N_\mu$ values, while gamma/hadron separation exploits a qualitative physical difference (gamma showers produce essentially zero muons).

\subsection{Haiku vs.\ Opus: Research Strategy Comparison}

The two agents exhibited distinctly different research styles (\Cref{tab:agent_comparison}).

\begin{table}[H]
\caption{Behavioral comparison between Claude Opus~4.6 and Claude Haiku~4.5 as autonomous ML researchers.\label{tab:agent_comparison}}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lCC}
\toprule
\textbf{Dimension} & \textbf{Opus~4.6} & \textbf{Haiku~4.5} \\
\midrule
Research style & Depth-first & Breadth-first \\
Architecture families tried & 5 & 9+ \\
Experiments per session & 12 (focused) & 3--41 (variable) \\
Total experiments & $\sim$57 & $\sim$97 \\
Crash rate & Low & Moderate \\
Session stability & Continuous & Idle every 10--15~min \\
Ensemble strategy & Same-architecture & Cross-architecture \\
Key innovation & SAM + DE synergy & Cross-arch. ensemble \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\textbf{Opus} exhibited a \textit{depth-first} strategy: after identifying CNN+Attention+MLP as the best architecture, it systematically explored 9 training modifications within that family. This led to the non-obvious discovery that SAM optimization, despite not having the best raw score, produces confusion matrices most amenable to DE post-processing. Opus also showed stronger software engineering: fewer crashes, cleaner code, and better artifact management.

\textbf{Haiku} exhibited a \textit{breadth-first} strategy: it tried 9+ architecture families including unconventional approaches (autoencoders, contrastive learning, isolation forests) that Opus never attempted. While most of these failed, the architectural diversity eventually led to the key insight that cross-architecture ensembles capture complementary signal patterns. Haiku's code was less reliable (more shape mismatches, OOM errors), but its willingness to explore dead ends paradoxically led to its most important finding.

Both agents converged on CNN+Attention as the best single-model architecture, suggesting this architectural insight is robust across agent capabilities. The complementarity of their strategies --- Opus's systematic optimization and Haiku's exploratory breadth --- suggests that multi-agent approaches combining both styles could be productive.

\subsection{Agent vs.\ Human Research Patterns}

Comparing the agents' approaches with the human researchers who developed the published baselines~\cite{kascade_jinst,kascade_icrc}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Breadth}: Across all runs, agents tested 10+ architecture families and numerous training strategies. Human researchers typically focus on 1--2 architectures over weeks to months.
    \item \textbf{Speed}: Agents conducted $\sim$154 experiments in approximately 50 total GPU-hours. Equivalent human experimentation would likely span weeks.
    \item \textbf{Systematic evaluation}: Agents evaluated every experiment with the same metric and evaluation script, enabling direct comparison. Human papers often report different metrics across experiments.
    \item \textbf{Failure documentation}: The agents' full trajectories --- including crashes and dead ends --- are preserved. Human papers report only successful results.
    \item \textbf{Cross-pollination}: Within a single session, agents applied insights from one approach to another (e.g., log1p preprocessing discovered for CNNs was applied to ViTs). Across sessions, insights accumulated through the research journal.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}

This study has several limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Agents operated with occasional human guidance on strategic direction (e.g., ``try GNNs'', ``fix the data pipeline''). Fully autonomous operation without any human intervention was not tested.
    \item Results are on simulation data only. Transfer to real KASCADE measurements requires validating that models trained on simulation generalize to real detector responses.
    \item The composition improvement (2.3\%) is modest, and the confusion matrix ceiling analysis suggests much larger gains require fundamentally different approaches.
    \item The two tasks were tackled by different agents (Opus for composition, Haiku for gamma), making it difficult to fully disentangle task difficulty from agent capability. Cross-task agent comparisons would strengthen the behavioral analysis.
    \item Haiku's gamma results benefited from accumulated knowledge across 4 sessions (77 total experiments), while Opus's composition results came primarily from 12 experiments on the matched pipeline. The total ``research effort'' is not directly comparable.
\end{itemize}


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have demonstrated that AI agents can serve as autonomous ML researchers on real astrophysics classification problems, improving upon published human-designed solutions on both tasks. On 5-class mass composition, Claude Opus~4.6 achieved a fraction error of 0.1045 (vs.\ published 0.107, a 2.3\% improvement) through systematic optimization of a CNN+Attention architecture with SAM and DE post-processing. On gamma/hadron separation, Claude Haiku~4.5 achieved a hadronic survival rate of $3.21 \times 10^{-4}$ at 75\% gamma efficiency --- approximately an order of magnitude better than published baselines --- through a cross-architecture ensemble discovered via broad exploratory search.

Across 9 runs and $\sim$154 experiments, we observe two complementary research strategies: Opus's depth-first systematic optimization within a chosen architecture, and Haiku's breadth-first exploration across many architecture families. Both strategies produced results that improve upon human baselines, but through different mechanisms: Opus found non-obvious training strategy synergies (SAM + DE), while Haiku discovered that architecturally diverse ensembles capture complementary signal patterns. Both agents independently converged on CNN+Attention as the best single-model architecture.

Beyond the classification results, the agents' research trajectories --- their decisions, pivots, failures, and insights --- constitute data for understanding how AI agents approach scientific problems. We make all code, model weights, training logs, and agent reasoning traces publicly available at \url{https://github.com/vvsotnikov/astro-bench}.

As AI agents become more capable, benchmarks like astro-bench that capture not just what was achieved but \textit{how} --- and that enable comparison across different agent models --- will be essential for understanding and improving the role of AI in scientific discovery.
